Kalshi Sentiment Predictor

An LLM-driven AutoML loop searching for predictive signal in Kalshi prediction markets, starting from transformer sentiment scores. It writes its own feature hypotheses from the literature and from its own history, then has to get them past a gate built to reject coincidence. The search procedure is the contribution.

The question

Does news sentiment carry residual signal a market hasn't priced in?

Prediction markets are supposed to absorb public information quickly, so if freely available news sentiment still predicts short-term price moves, that is a small crack in market efficiency. That is the domain question. The one this page is about is methodological: can an LLM-driven loop decide for itself which feature families are worth trying, and can its own accept-or-reject machinery be trusted to tell signal from noise?

The loop

Each round pitches three competing candidates and keeps at most one.

The pitch is written from the trial history, so what has already failed steers the next round as much as what has worked. The gate in the fourth stage is deliberately hard to pass.

The discovery claim

The loop opened a feature family the catalog does not contain.

Most AutoML searches a space someone drew first: a list of candidate transforms, a grid, a set of columns to cross. This one has such a list — seven feature families, every one of them derived from sentiment, written out by hand. What matters is that the loop did not stay inside it. The reasoning agent reads the literature and a structured record of every iteration it has already run, then writes a hypothesis: a feature family it thinks will carry signal, and why. A trial subagent turns that sentence into feature code in an isolated worktree. Partway through, unprompted, it began proposing market-structure and calendar features, computed from nothing but the event timestamp or the market ticker, and spent 12 of its 59 trials there.

It ran 59 iterations under 5-fold temporal CV and kept 7 of them. The other 52 were logged as neutral or regressed and written back into the history with their reasoning, so the next iteration moves somewhere new instead of re-proposing what already failed. A search that rejects 88% of its own ideas is doing the work; the rejections are the record.

Those 59 were judged on mean CV F1 clearing best + 0.005. The fold-to-fold spread at the time was ±0.031, wide enough that a mean-only bar is close to a coin flip, so the gate was later tightened: a candidate now has to beat the baseline on 4 of 5 folds as well. The first thing the paired rule caught was a non-result. Making fold membership reproducible moved CV from 0.3848 to 0.3906 on row ordering alone — +0.0058, clearing the mean bar — and the paired check scored it 3 of 5 folds and logged it NEUTRAL. The same fix cut the spread to ±0.0161.

That discipline is what makes the negative result worth reporting. A loop that searched a feature space it partly wrote itself, under temporal cross-validation, and could not clear a momentum baseline, has established something firmer than a loop that got lucky: it marks where sentiment stops paying, and it says so with a gate strict enough that the boundary can be trusted.

Future work

What the paper says to do next.

Three changes, each aimed at a specific way this run was weaker than it should have been.

  1. 01

    Use temporal cross-validation from the first iteration

    The early loop scored every candidate against one fixed validation split and peaked at F1 = 0.373 there. Re-scored under 5-fold temporal CV, the same feature set was worth 0.333. Temporal CV was adopted mid-project as a repair; starting there would have made every iteration's number comparable and caught the overfit far earlier.

  2. 02

    Put price-based features in the initial feature set

    Rolling volatility, momentum, and bid-ask proxies were never given to the loop as starting material, so it searched sentiment space alone while the baseline it had to beat was itself pure momentum. Seeding the price features lets the search find sentiment signal conditional on them rather than competing against them.

  3. 03

    Optimize for statistical alpha, not raw F1 gain

    F1 is the wrong objective for a market question. A model that is rarely confident but well calibrated when it is — exactly what the confidence-gated result describes — scores poorly on average F1 while being the more useful object. Restructuring the AutoML objective toward statistical alpha would search directly for that.
