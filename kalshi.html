An LLM-driven AutoML loop searching for predictive signal in Kalshi prediction markets, starting from transformer sentiment embeddings. It writes its own feature hypotheses from the literature and from its own history, then has to get them past a gate built to reject coincidence. The search procedure is the contribution.

The question

Does news sentiment carry residual signal a market hasn't priced in?

Prediction markets are supposed to absorb public information quickly, so if freely available news sentiment still predicts short-term price moves, that is a small crack in market efficiency. That is the domain question. The one this page is about is methodological: can an LLM-driven loop decide for itself which feature families are worth trying, and can its own accept-or-reject machinery be trusted to tell signal from noise?

The loop

A reasoning agent proposes; trial subagents write the code.

Each iteration is a hypothesis with a paper trail. The acceptance gate is the part that matters — it is deliberately hard to pass.

The discovery claim

The search space was never enumerated by a human.

What autonomy means here

Most AutoML searches a space someone drew first: a list of candidate transforms, a grid, a set of columns to cross. This one does not have that list. The reasoning agent reads the literature and a structured record of every iteration it has already run, and writes a hypothesis — a feature family it thinks will carry signal, and why. A trial subagent turns that sentence into feature code in an isolated worktree. No human decided in advance which families were eligible.

Which makes the gate the load-bearing part. An agent free to propose anything will propose noise, and against a fold-to-fold spread of ±0.031 a mean-improvement threshold would wave that noise through as discovery. Requiring a candidate to clear best + 0.005 on the mean and beat the baseline on 4 of 5 folds is what converts a proposal into evidence. Everything rejected is written back into the history with its reasoning, so the next iteration moves somewhere new instead of re-proposing what already failed.

That discipline is what makes the negative result worth reporting. A loop that searched an unsupervised feature space under purged temporal cross-validation, and could not clear a momentum baseline, has established something firmer than a loop that got lucky: it marks where sentiment stops paying, and it says so with a gate strict enough that the boundary can be trusted.

Future work

What the paper says to do next.

Three changes, each aimed at a specific way this run was weaker than it should have been.

  1. 01

    Use temporal cross-validation from the first iteration

    The early loop ran on a fixed validation split, peaked at F1 = 0.373, and collapsed to 0.333 on test. Temporal CV was adopted mid-project as a repair. Starting there would have made every iteration's number comparable and caught the overfit far earlier.

  2. 02

    Put price-based features in the initial feature set

    Rolling volatility, momentum, and bid-ask proxies were never given to the loop as starting material, so it searched sentiment space alone while the baseline it had to beat was itself pure momentum. Seeding the price features lets the search find sentiment signal conditional on them rather than competing against them.

  3. 03

    Optimize for statistical alpha, not raw F1 gain

    F1 is the wrong objective for a market question. A model that is rarely confident but well calibrated when it is — exactly what the confidence-gated result describes — scores poorly on average F1 while being the more useful object. Restructuring the AutoML objective toward statistical alpha would search directly for that.
